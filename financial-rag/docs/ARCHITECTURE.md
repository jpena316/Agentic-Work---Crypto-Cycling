# How This System Works

A plain-language explanation of the four design decisions behind the Financial Research Assistant, written for the people who will use it to research VRT and SNOW filings — not for the engineers who built it.

## Chunking strategy

Before a filing or transcript can be searched, it has to be broken into smaller pieces, because the underlying AI models can only "read" a limited amount of text at once. The naive way to do this is to slice the document every N words, the way you might cut a ribbon into equal lengths without looking at what's printed on it. We didn't do that. Instead, the system reads the document's own structure first — the "Item 1. Business," "Item 1A. Risk Factors," "Item 7. MD&A" headers in a 10-K, or the named speaker turns in an earnings call transcript ("Jimmy Sexton — CFO: ...") — and only then splits within those boundaries, respecting sentence endings so no piece stops mid-thought. The payoff is that when the system retrieves a piece of text to answer your question, that piece is still recognizably "Vertiv's Risk Factors section" or "the CFO's remarks on gross margin," not an orphaned fragment that happens to start in the middle of one paragraph and end in the middle of another. That's also what makes trustworthy citations possible: every citation you see traces back to a real, named section of a real filing, not an arbitrary slice.

## Embedding model

To make text searchable by meaning rather than by exact keyword match, every chunk is converted into a list of numbers — an "embedding" — using an OpenAI model, even though the question-answering itself is done by Anthropic's Claude. This isn't a contradiction; it's a division of labor. Turning text into a searchable numeric representation and generating a fluent written answer are different jobs, and OpenAI's embedding models are a well-established, inexpensive, purpose-built tool for the first one, while Claude is the stronger writer and reasoner for the second. Within OpenAI's embedding lineup we chose the "small" model over the "large" one. The large model is marginally more precise, but for a corpus of two companies' filings and transcripts — not millions of documents spanning every industry — that precision gain is not worth its higher per-query cost and slower response time. Small is the right-sized tool for a corpus this focused.

## Cosine vs. dot product

Once two pieces of text are each represented as a list of numbers, the system needs a way to measure how "similar in meaning" they are. Cosine similarity asks: do these two pieces of text point in the same conceptual direction, regardless of how long or short either one is? It's the right question for this use case, because a two-sentence question like "What are Vertiv's main revenue streams?" should be able to match strongly against a much longer paragraph from the filing, as long as that paragraph is substantively about the same thing — length shouldn't be held against it. Dot product, by contrast, blends direction together with magnitude, so it rewards not just topical closeness but also sheer length or emphasis — useful in some search contexts, but here it would risk favoring long, dense chunks purely because they're long and dense, rather than because they're the best answer. Cosine keeps the comparison focused on meaning rather than size.

## Grounding and its limits

Every answer this system produces is generated with an explicit instruction to Claude: use only the retrieved excerpts provided, mark every claim with a citation number, and say plainly that the information isn't in the documents if it truly isn't there. This meaningfully reduces the risk of the model inventing facts or drifting onto outside knowledge, and the citations let you verify any claim yourself by checking exactly which section it came from — that verifiability is the system's real safeguard, not blind trust in the model's obedience. But it's worth being direct about what this setup is and isn't: it's a strong instruction the model is asked to follow, not a mechanical guarantee enforced by the software. It's also only as good as what was retrieved — if the search step misses the most relevant passage in a filing, the model will honestly answer from what it was given, which may still be an incomplete picture even though every citation it cites is accurate. Treat every answer as a well-sourced starting point for your own reading of the underlying filing, not as a final, audited conclusion.
